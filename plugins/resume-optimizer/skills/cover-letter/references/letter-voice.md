# Letter Voice

## Length

250–400 words of letter body, three to four paragraphs, one page. A cover letter that runs
long does not read as thorough. It reads as unedited.

## Openings that are never used

- "I am writing to apply for…"
- "I am writing in response to…"
- "Please accept my application for…"
- "My name is…"
- Any first clause whose subject is the job posting rather than the candidate.

## Phrases that are never used

"I believe I would be a great fit", "team player", "fast learner", "passionate about",
"perfect candidate", "wealth of experience", "hit the ground running", "as you can see
from my resume", "I am confident that", "detail-oriented", "results-driven", "proven track
record", "I would love the opportunity to".

Every one of these is a claim with no evidence attached, phrased so it cannot be checked.
Replace each with the specific thing that made the candidate say it, or cut it.

## Patterns that give away a generated draft

A letter can avoid every phrase above and still read as machine-written, because the
strongest tells are structural rather than lexical. These are the ones that surface in
application letters, drawn from Wikipedia's
["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

- **Superficial `-ing` clauses.** A trailing participle that restates the sentence as
  significance: "I rebuilt the ingest pipeline, demonstrating my commitment to data
  quality." The clause carries no fact the sentence did not already have. Cut it, or
  replace it with the result.
- **Negative parallelism.** "Not just a rewrite, but a rethink." "It wasn't about speed,
  it was about trust." The shape promises a distinction and delivers a mood. Say the
  second half and drop the first.
- **Promotional register.** Language that sells rather than reports: *cutting-edge,
  seamless, robust, invaluable, deep dive, leverage, spearheaded, showcase, testament to*.
  A letter that reports what happened persuades better than one advertising it.
- **Vague attribution.** "Industry leaders recognise", "it is widely known", "many would
  argue". Name the source or cut the sentence.
- **Hedging on the candidate's own record.** "I believe", "arguably", "I feel I may have",
  "it could be said". Hedging your own history reads as doubt about it. State it plainly
  or leave it out.
- **Filler openers.** "It is worth noting that", "when it comes to", "in today's
  landscape", "in the world of". Every one of these can be deleted without loss.
- **Generic positive conclusions.** A closing paragraph that gestures at enthusiasm and
  impact while naming nothing. [letter-structure.md](letter-structure.md) gives the
  conclusion four specific jobs; a paragraph doing none of them is filler in the most
  expensive position in the letter.
- **Rule of three as default rhythm.** Three adjectives, three clauses, three examples,
  paragraph after paragraph. An occasional triad is ordinary writing. A letter built on
  them is not.
- **Elegant variation.** Cycling synonyms for one thing to avoid repeating a word: *the
  company*, *the organisation*, *the firm*, *the team*. Repeat the word. Readers track it,
  they do not tire of it.
- **Em and en dashes.** Cut them. A comma, a full stop, or a colon does the same work
  without the signature.

Where these collide with Register below, Register wins. A dossier that is genuinely funny,
or an employer type that invites personality, is no licence to reinstate promotional
language, but it does outrank a mechanical hunt for triads and participles. The goal is a
letter that sounds like the candidate, not one sanded until it sounds like nobody.

## Register

The source guide's own sample letters take real tonal risks — a parenthetical aside about
a childhood, a rhetorical question as a closing line. That is deliberate. The cover letter
is the one document in the application where the candidate sounds like a person, and
sanding it into corporate neutral removes the only thing it had that the résumé did not.

Write in the candidate's voice as their own material shows it. Contractions are fine.
Short sentences are fine. Humour is fine when the candidate is funny in their dossier.

Adapt the register to the employer, and **say so in Choices Made when you do**:

| Employer type | Register |
|---|---|
| Early-stage startup, creative agency, small studio | Closest to the guide's samples; personality forward |
| Large private company, consultancy | Warm but composed — one moment of voice, not four |
| Government, defence, healthcare, regulated finance, academia | Plain and direct; the hook is a concrete fact rather than a flourish |

When you dial the register down, name it, so the candidate can push back if they know the
team better than the posting does.

## The feeling rule

Never assert an emotion or a history the candidate has not expressed.

"I was glad to see this posting" is fine — ordinary interest is implied by applying. "I
have admired your work for years" is a claim about the candidate's life, and belongs in
the letter only if it is in their dossier or they just told you. The line is drawn case by
case in [company-research.md](../../../shared/company-research.md).

## Person and tense

First person, active voice. Past tense for what was done, present for what is true now.
No future-tense promises about performance — "I would quickly become an asset" is an
unevidenced claim wearing a different hat.

## The revision pass

Never ship a first draft. After drafting the letter and before validating it, run one
revision pass in three steps:

1. Read the draft as if reading it aloud. Vary sentence length, prefer the specific detail
   over the summarising abstraction, and let plain verbs stand — *is*, *has*, *built*,
   *ran*. Straining to avoid them is itself a tell.
2. Ask two questions and answer both briefly: **"What in this still reads as
   generated?"** and **"Does the revision state any fact, name, number, date, or
   relationship that is not in the candidate's material?"**
3. Revise into a final version that answers the first question and clears the second.

The second question is the one that earns its place here. Rewriting for naturalness is
exactly where a paraphrase inflates: *supported the migration* drifts to *drove the
migration*, *a few months* rounds to *a year*, *used the tool* becomes *owned the tool*.
A revision that sounds more human but adds a claim is a worse draft, not a better one,
because it fails in the direction the reader cannot detect and the interview will.

This pass narrows what reaches validation. It does not replace it — the checks in
[non-fabrication-letter.md](non-fabrication-letter.md) still run afterwards on the final
text.
