# Critical Review and Candidate Interview

Rewriting sentences is the smallest part of the job. A résumé usually loses interviews for
structural reasons — the strongest evidence is buried three bullets down, a section carries
weight it has not earned, the work that matches the posting is described in the candidate's
internal vocabulary, or the achievement that would win the screen was never written down at
all. Fix those before polishing wording.

Two limits govern everything here. Critique may propose anything; it may never manufacture
evidence — the never-invent list in
[evidence-rules.md](../../../shared/evidence-rules.md) still binds every suggestion. And
"restructuring" never means reordering the résumé's sections: the section order is fixed by
the MCS template layout in [writing-guide.md](writing-guide.md). Restructure *within* that
frame — which entries lead, which bullets appear and in what order, which section an item
belongs in, how many lines each item gets.

## Go past the rewrite

Propose all four kinds of change, not just the first:

- **Additions** — evidence the candidate has but did not put on the page: a project, a
  promotion listed as one flat role, an outcome mentioned in passing in the dossier, a
  publication or talk, ownership that the current wording hides.
- **Removals** — content that costs a line and returns nothing for *this* posting: stale
  technologies, a course listed as experience, duties that describe the job rather than the
  candidate, an entire item whose only function is completeness.
- **Restructuring** — moving an item to the section where it actually reads as strong (a paid
  contract sitting under Projects; a substantial course project buried under Education), or
  reordering entries and bullets so the first line a screener reads is the one that matches.
- **Refactoring** — splitting one overloaded bullet into two that each land, merging three
  thin ones into a single substantial claim, or rewriting a whole entry around a different
  organizing idea (the outcome, not the task list).

State each proposal concretely — the specific item, the specific change, and what it buys.
"Consider strengthening your projects" is not a proposal; "move the inventory tool out of
Projects into Experience, because you were paid for it and the posting weights production
work" is.

## Interrogate each section

For every section that carries real weight for this role, work through:

- What is weak, vague, or unclear here?
- How could this specific point stand out more than it currently does?
- What should the candidate be highlighting that they are not?
- Is something valuable buried, or given fewer lines than its importance deserves?
- What single additional detail would materially strengthen this section?

Report the answers that lead to a change or a question. Do not narrate the checklist section
by section when nothing came of it.

Look specifically for the undersold: an experience described in one thin bullet that the
dossier shows was substantial, technical depth flattened into a tool list, a scope or
ownership signal (team size, users, budget, on-call, design authority, who else could have
done this work) that exists in the source but never reached the page.

## Screen it as the hiring manager

Before returning the résumé, read it once as the person hiring for this exact role, with a
stack of other applications on the desk and a minute to spend. Say plainly:

- **What catches attention** — what would make this one worth a second read.
- **What makes you hesitate** — the gap, the ambiguity, the thing that reads as thin, the
  claim you would probe in a screen. Name it even when it is uncomfortable; a hesitation the
  candidate hears here is one they can answer before a recruiter reaches it silently.
- **What feels missing** — what you expected for this role and did not find.
- **What should be impossible to overlook** — the candidate's single strongest match for this
  posting, and whether the current page actually makes a six-second scan land on it.

Keep it in that voice: a screener's reaction to this posting and this page, not generic
résumé advice. Ground every point in something that is on the page or absent from it.

## Interview before you write

Where a probing question could unlock better evidence, ask it — do not quietly write around
the gap. Good questions are targeted and answerable from memory:

- Scale and outcome — "roughly how many users/records/requests?", "did that cut time,
  errors, or cost, and by about how much?"
- Ownership — "did you design that, or implement someone else's design?", "who else worked on
  it, and which parts were yours?"
- Technical depth — "what made that hard?", "what did you choose it over, and why?"
- Recognition — "was it shipped, adopted, published, presented, or awarded?"
- Missing history — work, projects, or coursework the posting asks about that the source
  material never mentions.

Ask the highest-value questions up front, when the answer would change what gets written or
what leads the page. Do not block on them: write the strongest version supported by the
current evidence, and carry the unanswered ones into Information Requests, each with what it
would unlock ("a user count here would turn the strongest bullet in Experience from a task
description into an impact claim"). If the user answers later, revise.

Never phrase a question so that it suggests its own answer, and never treat silence as
permission to estimate. An unanswered question means the bullet ships without the metric —
the missing-metric protocol in [non-fabrication.md](non-fabrication.md).
